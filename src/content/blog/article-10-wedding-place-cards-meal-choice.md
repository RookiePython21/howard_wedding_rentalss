# Wedding Place Cards With Meal Choice: A Simple Guide

Your caterer just asked how they'll know who ordered the chicken.

And you realized that the answer — "I have a spreadsheet" — is not going to help a server carrying two plates across a dark reception hall.

This is what wedding place cards with meal choice are for. They're the small, quiet system that lets your catering staff serve 140 plated dinners without ever asking a guest to raise their hand.

Take a deep breath. This is one of the easier problems on your list, and you can settle it in one sitting.

---

## Why Meal Indicators Exist

At a plated dinner, servers come out of the kitchen carrying specific plates. Beef here, salmon there, the vegetarian option two seats down.

Without a marker at each setting, the room turns into a call-and-response. "Who had the chicken?" Forty times. It slows service, it interrupts conversation, and it makes a plated dinner feel like a cafeteria line.

A meal indicator on the place card removes all of it. The server reads the card, sets the plate, moves on.

Here is the truth: your guests should never notice this system exists. That's what a good one looks like.

## The Four Ways to Mark a Meal Choice

There's no single right answer. Pick the one that fits your stationery and your caterer.

### 1. A small printed icon

A tiny symbol in the corner of the card. A sprig for vegetarian, a fish, a small steer or wheat stalk for beef.

**Why it works:** it's discreet, it prints cleanly, and it survives a dim room.

**Watch out for:** icons that look alike at a glance. A server reading fast at candlelight needs shapes that are obviously different, not two similar line drawings.

### 2. A colored element

A colored dot, a printed border, or a band of color at the top of the card. One color per entrée.

**Why it works:** it's the fastest to read from a distance. Servers can sort a tray before they reach the table.

**Watch out for:** low light. Navy and black are indistinguishable at a candlelit reception. Choose colors that stay separate in dim conditions, and give your caterer a printed key.

### 3. A letter or number code

A small "B," "C," or "V" in the corner — or a discreet numeral.

**Why it works:** it's unambiguous and it costs nothing extra to print.

**Watch out for:** overlap with your table numbers. If your cards already carry a number, adding a second one confuses everyone. Use letters instead.

### 4. Ribbon, charm, or wax seal

A physical element attached to the card — a colored ribbon tie, a small charm, a stamped seal.

**Why it works:** it's genuinely beautiful and it doubles as a design detail.

**Watch out for:** the labor. Someone has to tie 140 ribbons. That someone is usually you, your mother, or your maid of honor, at 11pm two nights before. Factor the hours in honestly before choosing this route.

**Your action:** ask your caterer which of these four they prefer. They serve weddings every weekend and they know what their team reads fastest. It's a two-line email and it settles the decision for you.

## What Goes on the Card

A place card with a meal choice still only needs a few things:

**The guest's name.** First and last, in whatever convention you're using across the whole set. Keep it consistent.

**The meal marker.** Small. Corner or edge. It should not compete with the name.

**Table number — sometimes.** Only if these cards are doing double duty as escort cards. If they're already sitting at the correct seat, the table number is redundant.

What does *not* go on the card: the full dish name. "Herb-Roasted Airline Chicken Breast with Pomme Purée" belongs on your menu card, not squeezed onto a 3.5-inch place card. The marker is for staff. The menu is for guests.

## Don't Forget the Dietary Restrictions

This is the piece that gets missed, and it matters more than the entrée choice.

Allergies and dietary needs are not the same as meal preference. A guest who ordered the salmon and a guest with a shellfish allergy need different handling, even if they're eating the same plate.

**How to handle it:**

Mark the standard entrée choice on the place card. Handle allergies separately — on a written list to your caterer and your venue coordinator, by name and by table.

Serious allergies should never rely on a symbol in the corner of a card. They should be a conversation between you, your caterer, and the kitchen, with the guest's table and seat written down.

**The benefit:** your caterer can plan the kitchen around it instead of improvising at service.

## Getting Your Counts to the Caterer

Your caterer needs two things, and they're both easy once your seating is set.

**A total count per entrée.** How many beef, how many chicken, how many vegetarian. This drives their ordering.

**A per-table breakdown.** Table four: three beef, five chicken, two vegetarian. This drives how they load trays.

Both come straight out of your seating chart. If you build your layout in [the free seating chart tool](https://howardweddingrentals.com/seating-chart-tool), you can export the arrangement and pull per-table counts from it without recounting anything by hand.

**Your action:** send your caterer the per-table breakdown at the same time you send your final headcount. Most caterers want this ten to fourteen days out. Sending it together saves an email round-trip.

## Timing: When to Actually Order

Sequence saves you from reprinting.

**Close RSVPs with the meal selection included.** Your RSVP card or online form should capture the entrée choice. If it didn't, you'll be making phone calls — start those early.

**Finalize the seating.** Meal markers are per-guest, and per-guest means you need to know where each guest sits.

**Verify the list out loud.** Read every name and every meal choice aloud with someone else checking against the RSVPs. Errors hide from your eyes. They don't hide from your ears.

**Order three to four weeks out.** That gives you printing and shipping time with room to breathe.

**Order a few blanks.** Late changes happen at every wedding. A small stack of unprinted cards and a decent pen handles them without drama.

## FAQ

**How do you indicate meal choice on a place card?**
The four common methods are a small printed icon, a color element, a discreet letter code, or a physical marker like a ribbon or wax seal. All four work — ask your caterer which their team reads fastest in low light, and go with that.

**Should the meal choice be visible to guests?**
It doesn't need to be hidden, but it should be subtle. The marker is functional information for your catering staff. Keep the guest's name as the visual focus of the card.

**What about guests with allergies?**
Handle allergies separately from entrée markers. Give your caterer and venue coordinator a written list of allergy guests by name and table. A symbol on a card is not enough for a serious allergy.

**How many extra place cards should I order?**
Order about five percent extra, or at least five blanks, whichever is more. Late RSVP changes and last-minute swaps are normal at every wedding.

**Can place cards work for a buffet reception?**
Yes, though meal markers aren't needed. For a buffet, place cards simply assign seats. The meal-choice marker only matters when servers are delivering specific plates to specific people.
