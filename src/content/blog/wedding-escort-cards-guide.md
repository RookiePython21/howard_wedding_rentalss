# Wedding Escort Cards: What They Are, How to Display

Take a deep breath. If "escort cards" showed up on a vendor checklist and you're not entirely sure what they are, you're in the right place — this term trips up almost every bride the first time she hears it.

Wedding escort cards are simpler than the name suggests. Here's what they do, how they differ from place cards, and how to display them well without turning it into its own design project.

---

## What a Wedding Escort Card Actually Is

An escort card is a small card, usually alphabetized and displayed near the entrance to your reception, that tells each guest which table to sit at. Guests find their name, note the table number, and walk in already knowing where they're headed.

That's the entire job: point a guest toward the right table. Escort cards don't assign an individual seat — that's what a place card does, once the guest actually reaches the table.

---

## Escort Cards vs. Place Cards: The Real Difference

This is where most of the confusion comes from, so let's cut through the noise.

An escort card answers "which table am I at?" A place card answers "which chair am I at?" They solve two different problems at two different moments of the reception — one at the door, one at the seat.

Some weddings use only escort cards, letting guests choose any open seat once they reach their assigned table. Others use both: an escort card at the entrance, then a place card waiting at the actual seat. Which combination you need depends on how much control you want over exact seating — escort cards alone if you just want people grouped correctly, both if specific pairings within a table matter to you.

Think of it as two separate decisions layered on top of each other. The first decision is whether guests are grouped into tables at all, which is where the escort card comes in. The second, optional decision is whether you also want to control exactly who sits next to whom within that table, which is what a place card adds. You can stop after the first decision and still have a well-organized reception — plenty of couples do.

## Do You Need Escort Cards at All?

Not every reception does. If your event is small, casual, or fully open seating, guests can simply pick a table themselves, and an escort card display becomes an extra step with no real benefit.

Escort cards earn their place when your guest list is large enough that "just find a seat" turns into standing around, or when you've deliberately grouped tables by family, friend group, or relationship — groupings your guests can't guess on their own.

The good news? This isn't a decision you have to make in isolation. It follows directly from whether you're using a seating chart at all. If you've already built one, escort cards are simply that chart's tabletop, guest-facing form.

## Escort Card Display Styles, Plainly Explained

**Alphabetized table.** The classic approach: a table near the entrance with cards arranged alphabetically by last name, easy for guests to scan quickly.

**Hanging or standing display.** Cards clipped to ribbon, tucked into a wood or wire frame display, or arranged on a board — more visual, and a nice complement if you're already using a printed seating chart board elsewhere at your reception.

**Individual favors.** Small items — a jar, an ornament, a mini bottle — each tagged with a guest's name and table number, so the escort card doubles as a take-home gift. Charming, but more assembly work than a flat card.

Whichever style you choose, keep the lettering large and the layout uncluttered. Guests often arrive in a cluster right after the ceremony, sometimes juggling a coat or a gift — a scannable, well-lit display keeps that moment calm instead of congested.

Lighting matters more than people expect, especially for an evening reception. A display that reads perfectly in afternoon light can turn nearly unreadable once the sun goes down and the room shifts to string lights or candles. If your ceremony and reception overlap that transition, a quick check of the display spot at the actual time guests will be using it is worth the five minutes it takes.

## A Few Small Mistakes to Avoid

Escort cards are simple by design, but a handful of small missteps can undo that simplicity fast.

**Putting the display somewhere guests miss it.** If the table is tucked in a corner or behind a doorway, guests will wander into the reception looking lost instead of confidently heading to their table. Put it somewhere directly in the path from ceremony to reception, not somewhere they have to search for.

**Making guests hunt through disorganized cards.** Alphabetizing matters more than it sounds like it should. A stack of cards in random order turns a thirty-second glance into a slow shuffle through a crowd, right when everyone is trying to move into the reception at once.

**Skipping a final name check.** Escort cards are printed from your guest list, so any last typo or nickname mismatch carries straight through. Do one final read-through against your RSVPs before printing, the same way you would for place cards.

**Treating the display as an afterthought.** Because escort cards feel like a small, mechanical step, it's tempting to throw them together last. But this is often the first physical thing guests interact with at your reception — worth the same care as any other tabletop detail, even if it only takes an extra few minutes to get right.

None of this needs to be complicated. It's a short checklist, not a redesign — and running through it once keeps the entrance to your reception feeling as calm as the rest of the evening.

## Ordering and Timing

Like place cards, escort cards depend on your final guest list and table assignments, so they're a late-stage stationery item, not an early one. Wait until RSVPs are in and your seating chart is close to final before ordering or printing.

Your action today: decide whether your reception is using assigned tables at all. If it is, escort cards, with or without place cards at the seat, are the way to communicate that plan. If it's fully open seating, you can cross this off your list entirely.

## FAQ

**Are escort cards and place cards the same thing?**
No. An escort card assigns a table; a place card assigns a specific seat at that table. Some receptions use one, some use both.

**Do I need escort cards for a small wedding?**
Usually not. Smaller, casual receptions often work fine with open seating and no escort card display at all.

**Can escort cards double as a small favor?**
Yes — many couples attach names and table numbers to small individual gifts, letting the escort card do double duty.

**Where should the escort card display go?**
Near the entrance to your reception space, somewhere guests naturally pass before finding a seat — a spot they can't miss on the way in.

**What if a guest's name is spelled wrong on their escort card?**
Fix it before the day if you catch it — double-check your list against RSVPs ahead of printing to avoid this altogether.

**Do escort cards need to match my place cards?**
They don't have to be identical, but matching fonts and colors across both keeps your entrance and your tables feeling like one cohesive look.
