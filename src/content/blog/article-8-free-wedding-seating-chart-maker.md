# Free Seating Chart Maker for Weddings: What Actually Works

You've got a guest list, a venue floor plan, and a growing sense that these two things are never going to agree with each other.

So you searched for a free seating chart maker. Smart move. There is no reason to pay for software you'll use for one afternoon.

But here's the catch: most of the free tools that come up were not built for weddings. They were built for classrooms, conferences, and office layouts. They'll get you *a* chart. They won't get you a chart that accounts for your divorced parents, your college roommate who doesn't know anyone, and the fact that table six is directly under a speaker.

Take a deep breath. Let's cut through the noise.

---

## What a Seating Chart Maker Actually Has to Do

Before comparing tools, it helps to know what you're asking one to do. Most brides need four things:

**Hold your guest list.** Names, plus-ones, kids, late RSVPs. It has to be easy to add someone on a Tuesday night without rebuilding anything.

**Match your real venue.** Round tables, long farm tables, a head table, a sweetheart table. If the tool only offers one shape, you'll spend your evening fighting it.

**Let you move people fast.** You will move guests around more times than you expect. Drag-and-drop is not a luxury here. It's the whole job.

**Give you something you can hand off.** Your caterer wants a count per table. Your venue coordinator wants a floor plan. Your calligrapher wants a clean name list. If the tool can't export, you're retyping everything.

That's the checklist. Now let's see how the free options hold up against it.

## The Free Options, Compared Honestly

### General design tools

Think of the big graphic design platforms with drag-and-drop canvases and template libraries. They're genuinely free, genuinely easy, and they produce something beautiful.

**What they're good at:** the *sign*. The pretty display board your guests read on the way in.

**Where they fall down:** they don't know what a guest is. A name on a table in a design tool is just text in a box. Move a guest and nothing recalculates. There's no headcount, no per-table total, no way to catch that you've seated Aunt Linda twice.

**Use them for:** designing the final display, after your seating is decided.

### Generic seating chart software

These exist, and some are free at the basic tier. They're usually built for classroom seating or conference halls.

**What they're good at:** grid layouts. Fixed rows. Assigning a person to a numbered seat.

**Where they fall down:** weddings aren't grids. You need round tables of eight and ten, a head table that seats an odd number, and a floor plan that bends around a dance floor. Most generic tools also gate the export behind a paid tier — which you'll discover after you've entered 140 names.

**Use them for:** honestly, not much, if your reception has round tables.

### A spreadsheet

Never underestimate this one. A spreadsheet is free, you already have it, and it will never crash on you.

**What it's good at:** the guest list itself. Tracking RSVPs, meal choices, dietary notes, and who still hasn't replied.

**Where it falls down:** it has no spatial sense. A spreadsheet can tell you that eight people are at table four. It can't show you that table four is jammed against the bar.

**Use it for:** your master guest list. Then move that list into something visual.

## Why Wedding Seating Is a Different Problem

Here is the truth: the software part of a seating chart is easy. The human part is what keeps you up at night.

A conference planner seats people alphabetically. You can't. You're solving for relationships — who hasn't spoken in three years, who will talk to anyone, which cousin needs to be near the door because she's leaving early with a toddler.

That's why tool choice matters more than it seems. A tool that fights you on the mechanics steals attention from the decisions that actually shape the night.

**Your action:** before you open any tool, write down your five hardest guests. The ones you already know will be tricky. Seat those five first, then build the rest of the room around them. Every seating chart gets easier after the hard part is done.

## How to Build Your Chart Without Losing a Weekend

A workable order of operations:

**1. Lock the guest list first.** Not the seating — the list. Names, spellings, plus-ones. If you seat people before RSVPs settle, you'll do the work twice.

**2. Get your venue's floor plan.** Ask for the actual table layout and count. Most venues have a standard configuration and will send it over. Confirm table size — eight-tops and ten-tops change your math completely.

**3. Place tables before people.** Build the empty room first. Dance floor, head table, bar, exits. Seeing the space empty makes the guest placement obvious in a way a blank list never does.

**4. Seat in groups, not individuals.** Families, friend circles, work colleagues. Drop whole groups onto tables, then adjust the edges. This is faster than placing 140 people one at a time.

**5. Do a comfort pass.** Walk the room mentally. Who's next to a speaker? Who's furthest from the bathroom? Who's stuck facing away from the toasts? Small fixes here matter more to guests than anything on the menu.

**6. Export and share.** Send the floor plan to your coordinator and the per-table counts to your caterer. Then stop looking at it.

## FAQ

**Is there a genuinely free seating chart maker with no catch?**
Yes, but read what "free" covers. Some tools are free to build and charge to export — which is the part you actually need. Check the export before you invest an evening entering names. Our seating chart tool is free through export, with no account required.

**Can I make a wedding seating chart in a spreadsheet?**
You can, and it's a great place to keep your guest list, RSVPs, and meal choices. What a spreadsheet can't do is show you the room. Most brides end up using both — a spreadsheet for the data, a visual tool for the layout.

**How far in advance should I make my seating chart?**
Start roughly three to four weeks before the wedding, once most RSVPs are in. Earlier than that and you'll rebuild it every time someone replies. Later than that and you're rushing your printed materials.

**What if guests change after I've printed everything?**
They will. Build in a small buffer: leave one or two open seats at a couple of tables, and hold off on printing place cards until about ten days out. Late changes are normal, not a failure of planning.
