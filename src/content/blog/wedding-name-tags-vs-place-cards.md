# Wedding Name Tags vs. Place Cards: Which Do You Need?

Take a deep breath. If you searched "wedding name tags" and landed here, you are probably staring at a stationery checklist that suddenly feels longer than it should.

Here is the truth: wedding name tags and place cards are not the same thing, and you almost certainly only need one of them. Let's cut through the noise together.

This guide walks through what each one actually does, when guests expect to see them, and how to order the version that fits your reception — without spiraling into eight browser tabs of stationery shops.

---

## The quick answer

**Wedding name tags** are stick-on or clip-on labels guests wear on their clothing, usually for a casual, mingling-heavy event where guests may not know each other.

**Wedding place cards** are small printed cards set at the reception table (often on the plate or at a place setting) that tell each guest where to sit.

Most weddings need place cards, not name tags. Name tags belong at reunions, conferences, and some rehearsal dinners — not at a seated reception with assigned tables. But there are a few situations where name tags earn their spot, and we will cover those too.

---

## What wedding name tags actually solve

Name tags exist to answer one question fast: "Who is this person?"

That question matters most when:

- Guests are meeting for the first time (a big blended-family wedding, a destination wedding with two friend groups that have never overlapped)
- The event is a mingling format — a cocktail hour, a welcome party, or a reception with no assigned seating
- You want to make small talk easier for older relatives or out-of-town guests who feel lost in a crowd

### What they don't solve

Name tags don't tell a guest where to sit. They don't help a caterer track a meal choice. And frankly, most brides find them a little too "networking event" for a wedding reception. If your event has assigned tables, a name tag is solving a problem you don't have.

---

## What wedding place cards actually solve

Place cards answer a different question: "Where do I sit, and at which table?"

They do the quiet, practical work of a seated reception:

- Guide guests to their table the moment they walk in, no awkward hovering by the entrance
- Prevent the slow-motion chaos of forty people trying to self-select seats
- Double as a small, personal touch — a name in your wedding's font, at a table you thought carefully about

If your reception has assigned seating (and most do), this is the piece you need. It is also the piece guests actually expect. Nobody attends a wedding wondering who everyone is by first name — but everyone glances at the table chart looking for their name.

## Escort cards, place cards, and name tags: the naming confusion

Three terms get tangled together constantly, so here is the plain-English version.

### Escort cards
Handed out (or displayed) near the entrance. Tell a guest which **table** to go to. No seat assignment within the table — just "Table 6."

### Place cards
Set directly at an individual seat, usually for plated dinners. Tell a guest which **exact chair** is theirs, sometimes with a meal choice noted for the caterer.

### Name tags
Worn on the person, not placed on a table. Tell *other guests* who someone is, regardless of seating.

Many couples use "place cards" as a catch-all for the first two, and that is fine — vendors will understand what you mean. The important distinction is between something a guest reads on a table (place or escort cards) and something a guest wears (name tags).

---

## So which one do you actually need?

Ask yourself two questions.

**Is seating assigned?**
If yes, you need place cards (or escort cards, depending on your table format). This is true for almost every plated or buffet reception with numbered tables.

**Do most guests already know each other?**
If your crowd is mostly family and longtime friends, skip name tags entirely — they will feel unnecessary and slightly awkward. If you are blending two large, unfamiliar friend groups or hosting a multi-day destination wedding where guests mingle across several events, name tags at the welcome party (not the reception itself) can genuinely help.

*The benefit:* you stop trying to solve two different guest-experience problems with one product, and you order exactly what the day needs.

## How to order place cards without the decision fatigue

Once you know you need place cards, keep the choice simple:

1. **Pick a size that matches your place setting.** Tent-fold cards (roughly 2×3.5 inches) are the standard and sit cleanly on a napkin or charger.
2. **Decide if you need meal choices printed.** If you are serving a plated dinner with more than one entrée option, ask your caterer whether they need a printed indicator (an icon, a letter, or the meal name) on the card itself.
3. **Match your day-of fonts.** Consistency between your place cards, seating chart display, and any signage reads as intentional, not as an afterthought.

## Common mistakes couples make with wedding stationery

Decision fatigue is real by the time you reach place cards — you've already picked a venue, a caterer, and a color palette. Here are the mistakes we see most often, so you can skip them.

### Ordering before the seating chart is final

This is the big one. Printing place cards before your table assignments are locked means reprints, wasted cardstock, and a last-minute scramble. Build your seating chart first. Order second.

### Choosing a font that's hard to read across a table

A beautiful script font can look stunning on an invitation and be nearly impossible to read at arm's length in low reception lighting. Keep the guest's name legible above everything else — this is a functional piece of stationery first, a design piece second.

### Skipping the caterer conversation

If you're serving a plated dinner with meal choices, your caterer needs a way to identify each guest's order at the table. A quick call to confirm how they want that marked — icon, initial, or written out — saves everyone a headache the day of.

### Adding name tags "just in case"

If you're not sure whether you need them, you probably don't. Name tags add a task (printing, assembling, setting out, guests remembering to put them on) for a problem most weddings don't actually have. Save that mental energy for something that matters more.

## FAQ

**Do I need both name tags and place cards?**
Rarely. Most weddings only need place cards for the reception. Name tags are worth adding only for a separate mingling event, like a welcome party with two unfamiliar friend groups.

**What if I don't have assigned seating at all?**
Some receptions use open seating with just table numbers displayed, skipping individual place cards. It works fine for casual, smaller weddings — but it does put more pressure on your seating chart signage to be clear.

**Can place cards double as favors?**
Yes. Many couples print place cards on something small guests keep, or pair them with a favor. It's a nice touch, but not required — plain and legible always beats elaborate and hard to read.

**When should I order place cards?**
Once your RSVPs are in and your seating chart is finalized, usually 3–4 weeks before the wedding. That gives you buffer for last-minute changes without rushing printing.
